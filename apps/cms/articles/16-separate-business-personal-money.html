In many family businesses the shop drawer is also the family wallet. School fees, groceries and a wedding gift all come from the day's takings. It feels natural, but it makes one thing impossible: knowing whether the business actually makes money.

Why mixing money hurts

  • You can't see real profit. A good month might just be a month when the family spent less from the drawer.
  • Cash goes missing on paper. Your books say there should be ₹40,000; the drawer has ₹22,000, and nobody knows where it went.
  • Tax and loans get harder. Banks and tax officers want clean business records. Mixed accounts raise questions you can't easily answer.
  • Decisions get emotional. Without clear numbers, it's hard to decide whether to expand, hire or cut back.

Step 1: a separate bank account and UPI ID

Open a current account in the business name. Route all business receipts there, including the UPI QR on your counter, and pay all business expenses from it. Keep your savings account for household spending only.

Step 2: pay yourself a fixed amount

Decide a monthly amount the family takes from the business, like a salary, and transfer it on a fixed date. In your books it is recorded as owner's drawings, not an expense. If the business has a great month, the extra stays in the business, or you take a planned bonus, rather than leaking out day by day.

Step 3: record every withdrawal

Life happens, and sometimes you will take cash from the drawer. When you do, record it as drawings the same day. Five seconds of recording saves hours of searching for "missing" cash later.

Step 4: no personal spending on the business card

If something personal is accidentally paid from the business account, record it as drawings straight away. Don't leave it sitting as an unexplained expense.

Step 5: review once a month

Look atQuestion to ask
Net profitDid the business earn more than the family took out?
Owner's drawingsAre we taking more than we planned?
Cash and bank balanceIs the cushion growing or shrinking?
Receivables and stockIs profit turning into cash, or getting stuck?

If drawings are regularly larger than profit, the business is slowly shrinking even if sales look healthy.

Build a reserve

Once business and personal money are separate, keep a reserve in the business, ideally two to three months of fixed costs. It covers slow seasons, a big order you need to buy stock for, or an emergency, without borrowing at high interest.
